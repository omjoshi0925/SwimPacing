## Result 1. Even pacing is optimal, and it is a theorem

Under a static energy budget with a velocity-only cost function, the four-split
problem is a convex program in split times whose unique global optimum is
perfectly even pacing. This holds for every `p > 1` and every `k`, `R`, `E0`.

```
closed form   T = 100.000016 s   v = [1.8288, 1.8288, 1.8288, 1.8288]
SLSQP         T = 100.000016 s   gap vs closed form = 7.3e-12 s
ODE simulator T = 100.000016 s   terminal reserve = -4.4e-11 J
KKT residual  = 2.4e-11          max split spread = 1.3e-06 s
```

`T* = 1:40.00`, a realistic time for the target population. This is `E0`
calibrated to that target, not a prediction of it.

**Answers secondary question 1: yes, even pacing is mathematically optimal**, in
the base model. Since real races are positively split, the base model is missing
something specific, which is the useful part.

## Result 2. The optimal shape is independent of the engine and the hull

Adding position-coupled economy decay gives the exact solution

```
ti proportional to wi^(1/p),     wi = 1 + beta_x * xbar_i / L
```

The optimal split distribution depends only on `beta_x` and `p`. It does not
depend on `k`, `R`, `E0`, `rho`, `C_D`, `A`, `eta_p` or `eta_g`.

Confirmed numerically. Sweeping every parameter over its full range and
re-optimizing:

| parameter | max departure from even pacing |
|---|---|
| `beta_x` | 2.9e-02 |
| `C_D` | 3.2e-08 |
| `A` | 1.9e-08 |
| `R` | 1.2e-08 |
| `eta_g`, `eta_p` | 1.1e-08 |
| `E0` | 9.8e-09 |
| `tau` | 9.4e-09 |
| `rho` | 8.7e-09 |

Everything except `beta_x` is at solver noise.

**Answers secondary question 4, and it is a negative answer.** The optimal pacing
strategy does *not* change with swimmer characteristics, as long as those
characteristics are aerobic capacity, anaerobic capacity, drag or efficiency. A
swimmer with a bigger anaerobic tank should not go out faster. They should go the
same shape, faster. Only the rate at which their economy decays changes the plan.

That is a sharp, falsifiable, slightly counterintuitive claim, and it is the most
interesting thing the project has produced so far.

*(tau updated 2026-08-31 from the exploratory 20 s to the measured 16.5 s,
Pessoa Filho et al. 2012; phi recalibrated per model. Shapes below are
unchanged where theory fixes them; M2/M4 shifted at the third decimal.)*

## Result 3. Oxygen kinetics slow the race but do not bend the shape

Total aerobic supply over a race is

```
AerobicSupply(T) = R [ T - tau (1 - e^{-T/tau}) ]
```

a function of race duration alone. Two profiles finishing in the same time
receive identical aerobic energy, so the terminal budget cannot distinguish them.

| `tau` (s) | `T` (s) | split fractions |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100.00 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |
| 20 | 105.55 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |
| 40 | 111.03 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |
| 60 | 115.74 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |
| 120 | 126.05 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |
| 200 | 134.92 | 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.2500 |

### RETRACTED: the path-constraint boundary

A previous version of this document reported that past `tau` ≈ 40 s the energy
path constraint binds and the optimum moves slightly off even. **That result was
an artifact of an undersized `E0`.**

It arose because `E0` was 35 kJ, which the drag-parameter correction raised to
50.32 kJ. With a correctly sized reserve the constraint is slack for every `tau`
tested up to 200 s, and the even-pacing result holds unconditionally at this
calibration.

The mechanism is real and still implemented and tested: a small reserve combined
with slow kinetics does still produce an infeasible terminal-constraint solution
(`E0` = 20 kJ at `tau` = 20 s, for instance). It just does not occur anywhere
near the calibrated parameters. Recorded rather than quietly deleted, because a
qualitative "result" vanishing when a parameter error was fixed is exactly the
kind of thing a reader should be told about.

## Result 4. Two fatigue mechanisms make opposite predictions

| Model | Mechanism | P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 | shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M0 | none | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | exactly even |
| M1 | oxygen kinetics | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | 0.2500 | exactly even |
| M2 | reserve-coupled cost | 0.2751 | 0.2589 | 0.2409 | 0.2251 | strong negative |
| M3 | position-coupled cost | 0.2422 | 0.2475 | 0.2527 | 0.2576 | linear positive |
| M4 | fatigue-coupled ceiling | 0.2398 | 0.2497 | 0.2541 | 0.2565 | front-loaded positive |

Three distinct qualitative predictions across five models. M3 and M4 are
separable by **shape**, not just magnitude: M4 puts most of the loss between the
first and second 50 (drop ratio about 4x M3's), so `drop_1_2 / drop_3_4` is a
sharper discriminator than aggregate RMSE. The pipeline computes it per race.

M0 and M1 make **identical** shape predictions and can never be separated by
split data. Report them as one shape hypothesis.

Reserve-coupled decay rewards holding back, because a full tank means good
economy, so the reserve is worth protecting for its own sake. Position-coupled
decay rewards banking speed early, because the same velocity is simply cheaper
early than late.

Real 200s are positively split even after crediting the dive start, which is
evidence against `beta_E` and in favour of `beta_x`. **This contrast should be
the backbone of the Phase 12 model comparison.**

## Result 5. Pacing penalties

Every strategy rescaled to finish with exactly zero reserve, so the penalty
measures spending the same fuel in a different order, not trying less hard.

| strategy | splits | `T` | penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| optimal / even | 25.00 25.00 25.00 25.00 | 1:40.00 | 0.000 s |
| positive split | 24.27 24.75 25.25 25.77 | 1:40.06 | +0.055 s |
| negative split | 25.77 25.25 24.75 24.27 | 1:40.06 | +0.055 s |
| conservative opening | 26.60 25.26 24.51 23.81 | 1:40.18 | +0.184 s |
| aggressive opening | 23.15 25.01 25.78 26.32 | 1:40.27 | +0.265 s |
| very aggressive | 21.76 25.03 26.34 27.81 | 1:40.94 | +0.936 s |

Opening penalty, with splits 2 to 4 re-optimized in every case:

| first 50 vs optimal | time lost |
|---|---|
| -6% | 0.136 s |
| -3% | 0.034 s |
| +3% | 0.033 s |
| +6% | 0.130 s |
| +10% | 0.357 s |

**Answers secondary question 3.** Opening too aggressively is expensive but not
catastrophic at realistic magnitudes. Going out 3% hot costs 0.03 s. Going out
15% hot costs about 0.9 s, which does decide races.

Three structural facts:

1. **The penalty is locally quadratic.** Doubling the opening error quadruples
   the cost. Small errors are nearly free, large ones are not.
2. **The objective is flat near the optimum.** This is why "just go out
   controlled" is decent advice and why precise pacing prescriptions are worth
   less than they sound.
3. **Under constant economy, too fast and too slow cost the same.** Symmetric
   errors give symmetric penalties to within 15%. Coaches would dispute this
   strongly, and it is a real testable weakness of the constant-economy model,
   not a rounding artifact. Note that the working model with `beta_x` active
   *does* break the symmetry: going out too slow costs 0.450 s while going out
   too fast costs 0.083 s, which matches coaching intuition much better.

## Result 6. What race time is actually sensitive to

Change in optimal `T` over a ±10% move in each parameter:

| parameter | ΔT | elasticity | moves the shape? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `eta_g` | -7.23 s | -0.361 | no |
| `eta_p` | -7.23 s | -0.361 | no |
| `A` | +7.21 s | +0.360 | no |
| `C_D` | +7.21 s | +0.360 | no |
| `R` | -5.64 s | -0.282 | no |
| `E0` | -1.57 s | -0.079 | no |
| `beta_x` | +0.89 s | n/a | **yes** |
| `tau` | +0.57 s | n/a | no |

`C_D`, `A` and `rho` enter only through their product, so their elasticities are
identical by construction. `rho` is excluded from the figure because a realistic
pool spans well under 1%, not the 10% plotted.

Efficiency and drag dominate race time. The anaerobic reserve matters
surprisingly little: a 10% larger tank is worth 1.6 s, while 10% less drag is
worth 7.2 s.

## Verification

102 tests across `tests/test_model.py` and `tests/test_pipeline.py`, all passing. They pin claims, not style.

- Three independent solvers (closed form, SLSQP in time space, ODE integration)
  agree to about 1e-11 s across a range of `beta_x`.
- The midpoint weighting is verified exact, not approximate, to 1e-6 relative.
- Physical plausibility: drag factor in the published band, race pace
  supramaximal, race time realistic for the population, anaerobic share of the
  working model between 30 and 50%.
- The even-pacing result is checked for `p` in {2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5} and across
  ten parameter perturbations.
- The path-constraint boundary at `tau = 45` and `tau = 60` is checked to be a
  real effect, with the relaxed solution confirmed to be a strict lower bound.

## Open questions going into Phase 9

1. **What is the dive start actually worth?** `START_OFFSET_S = 1.80` is the
   weakest number in the model and it sits directly on the main comparison. It
   needs to be measured, ideally from 15 m splits.
2. **What is `beta_x` when fitted rather than guessed?** If the fitted value is
   physiologically implausible, the model is absorbing the start into the fatigue
   parameter.
3. **Are the predicted penalties detectable?** A 5% opening error costs about
   0.09 s, inside the noise of a single swim. The empirical test needs many races
   and a well-specified expectation model, and it may come back null. That would
   be worth reporting.
4. **Do elite swimmers race closer to the optimum?** Secondary question 5, and
   the one most likely to produce a clean empirical finding, because it needs
   only a relative comparison rather than a calibrated absolute penalty.
